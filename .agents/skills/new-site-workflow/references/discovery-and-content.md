# Discovery and content planning

Read during Stage 1 for a new site or substantial change to its audience, offer or purpose, and during Stage 3 before drafting important page copy. Reuse current evidence and settled decisions on an existing site. This reference extends the workflow's question policy; it does not add a stage, required document or approval stop.

## Choose the useful depth

Start with supplied briefs, call notes, existing pages, reviews, approved research and assets. Identify what is known, contradictory or missing before asking. An existing site's claims are source material to check, not automatic evidence that those claims remain true or may be republished.

Use **core discovery** for a clear small brief: intended outcome, priority visitor, offer or service, next action, available evidence and delivery constraints. Use **deeper discovery** when positioning is unclear, audiences compete, the buying journey is complex or a redesign must address documented underperformance. Depth is independent of Express/Standard and platform; a small sales page can need deeper discovery, while a large migration can reuse a sound brief.

**Refinement requests start open.** When the owner asks to make an existing site "sleeker", "more modern" or similar, first ask what bothers them when they look at it, in their own words, before offering options. If you do offer options, span opposite directions (quieter and livelier); options built only on your own reading can send the work the wrong way (a "sleeker" request turned out to mean "less sterile, more fun"). Treat the adjective as unresolved until there is a feeling or an example.

**Or derive the brief from the built site.** When the site already has an accepted direction, an Art Direction Record and a current brief, and the owner asks only to polish it ("it feels basic", "spruce it up", "don't do discovery"), do not run a questionnaire. Write the diagnosis yourself instead: what reads as basic on the rendered pages, measured against the owner's own sites and stated references (flat grounds, small photography, repeated section skeletons, plain widgets), and name the fixed commitments you keep. Give it to the owner as one confirm-or-correct paragraph and go straight to concepts. Offer this path whenever discovery would only repeat answers the site already holds; ask open questions only where the diagnosis genuinely depends on the owner's feeling.

Record the owner's **working language** (the language for questions, briefs and review material) separately from the site's locales; a French-first site can have an English-speaking owner. Ask one to three related questions at a time, omitting answered questions. Follow a material answer one level deeper with a concrete example: a recent customer, an actual enquiry, an existing process or words the audience used. If a required answer is pending, wait before making the dependent decision and continue independent work. Summarize a consequential interpretation for correction; do not ask for confirmation of every answer. Unknowns remain unknown, with an owner or research task when needed.

## Interview prompts, selected by purpose

These are prompts to adapt, not a questionnaire to paste or a completeness score:

| Topic | Core question | Go deeper when it changes the work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outcome | What should visitors accomplish, and what would make the site useful? | Why now? What failed before? What observable result, baseline and review period would indicate improvement? Record unknown baselines; do not invent targets or add tracking by default. |
| Audience | Who needs this most, in what situation? | Who else influences the decision? What do they already know? Who is a poor fit? Capture actual audience language with its source; distinguish paraphrases from quotations. |
| Offer or service | What is provided, for whom, and what happens next? | Which offer takes priority? What does it cost, include and exclude? What makes a useful enquiry? What happens after a booking, purchase, download or application? |
| Alternatives | Why choose this approach? | What do visitors compare it with, including doing it themselves or doing nothing? Ask for defensible differences. Research a small relevant sample only when useful; label observations and strategic inferences separately. |
| Questions and hesitation | What must someone know before acting? | What questions recur, what creates doubt, and what evidence could answer it? Do not assume every hesitation belongs in an FAQ. |
| Voice and identity | How should the site sound and feel to these visitors? Who speaks, and as whom? | Ask for an example and its reason when adjectives are vague. Settle the first-person stance explicitly: a solo practitioner may speak as "I", as the studio name, or as an inclusive "we" ("you and us working together") — never an implied team that does not exist — and whether the owner wants to be personally visible (name, photo) at all. Settle visitor address where the language requires it (for example *vous*/*tu*). Capture words/claims/imagery to avoid and fixed brand commitments. Leave typography, tokens and composition to art direction. |
| Content and proof | What usable material exists, and who owns it? | Which examples, credentials, samples or results support the intended message? Can they be published? What is missing, who supplies it, and when does it affect drafting or launch? |

Adapt the vocabulary to the site's purpose. For a public information service, use task completion, reliable information, eligibility and access rather than forcing a sales funnel. A portfolio may establish credibility through the work itself. A local service may need location, availability and a clear enquiry; a complex business purchase may need several decision-makers and a longer consideration journey. Keep the workflow's editing, integration, accessibility, locale, timing, budget and hosting questions alongside business discovery rather than dropping operational constraints.

## Synthesize before designing

In the fact's established home (`AGENTS.md`, or an explicitly adopted optional `PROJECT.md`), record a concise interpretation:

- Priority audience and situation; offer/service; primary outcome and next action, with secondary journeys where real.
- Recommended positioning and lead message, connected to a defensible difference or useful visitor outcome.
- Strongest available evidence; biggest uncertainty/risk; missing material and its owner.
- Voice and factual constraints; scope boundaries; the basis for assessing usefulness after launch.

Separate supplied facts, user-approved decisions, agent recommendations and working assumptions. Do not call a recommendation approved merely because it was saved. Ask directly about an unresolved choice that changes the work; otherwise continue the authorized next stage with labeled reversible assumptions. For marketing copy, establish audience, offer and page purpose before drafting; missing testimonials alone do not block honest drafts. Respect an explicit discovery-only or review-before-design scope.

## Plan important pages before composition

During Stage 3, select the representative route that exercises the most important journey or uncertainty; it need not be the homepage. In existing page notes or the route inventory, give each materially different priority page a compact brief:

| Page brief field | Capture |
| --- | --- |
| Visitor and arrival context | Who arrives, what they already know, and the relevant entry point or search intent |
| Page purpose | What they should understand or accomplish; the main next action, its route/destination and what follows; distinguish a planned page from any unresolved form-delivery endpoint |
| Lead message | The specific promise or useful answer, within the available evidence |
| Question coverage | The important visitor questions/objections and where each is answered |
| Section argument | Ordered visitor question → intended answer/message → supporting evidence → section purpose → next action where needed |
| Copy and material | Draft headings/body/action labels, source links, content owner, assets and approved/draft/missing status |

Do not fill every cell for every repeated entry. Share a brief for a route archetype, then note material exceptions. Give supporting pages their own task rather than repeating a conversion banner everywhere. Draft the representative page's actual copy before detailed composition; use realistic draft length so layout is tested against content. Alternatives for an important headline are useful when the strategic angle is unresolved, not a mandatory variant count.

Map the section argument to the concrete layout/pacing recipes in `astro-site-design` after its purpose is clear. Preserve the Art Direction Record and Stage 4.5 calibration. Keep those recipes as actionable defaults; document a specific content-led exception when a section order, layout or proof treatment does not fit. A low-fidelity composition is a planning artifact, not a passing visual gate or implemented route.

If requested, keep standalone low-fidelity HTML concepts isolated under `audit/visual-concepts/`, following the project's concept exception. Label draft/synthetic material and planned links whose destination pages are not included. Keep editorial evidence notes separate from customer-facing copy; explain unimplemented delivery rather than simulating a working submission. This optional artifact does not require a full site build or establish route acceptance.

## Claims, evidence and missing proof

For consequential marketing claims, maintain a compact record beside the relevant copy or in the existing evidence inventory: **claim → source → permitted use/status → owner → intended placement**. Reuse source identities across pages. Preserve restrictions such as private-only results, anonymous use, expired terms, unknown permission or an unverified historical claim.

Do not invent testimonials, audience quotations, client logos, measurements, credentials, guarantees, scarcity or delivery promises. A missing fact is not a reversible creative assumption. If evidence is thin, propose a real work sample, process demonstration, documented qualification or clear scope explanation instead. Omit unsupported proof sections rather than manufacturing a logo/metric strip. Draft wording and illustrative assets remain visibly identified as drafts; fictional rehearsal material must remain labeled synthetic and must not enter a real site's content or publication pipeline.

## Review the representative result

When actual participant research is requested or an important visitor-task uncertainty remains, use [optional visitor research](visitor-research.md) for neutral tasks and evidence-linked synthesis. Do not treat the owner/agent review below as a participant study or add a research checkpoint to a settled brief.

Alongside browser, accessibility and editor checks in Stages 5–6, inspect the actual representative page against its brief:

- Can a first-time visitor identify the offer/service, audience fit and intended next step from the rendered content?
- Are priority questions answered where needed, with clear scope, costs or eligibility where relevant?
- Do consequential claims match their evidence and permitted use? Are missing materials and draft publication status still explicit?
- Does the primary action lead to the promised destination, with a clear explanation of what follows and a usable failure/recovery path when applicable?

Record concrete observations and repairs with existing acceptance evidence. A reviewer or agent clarity pass is not user research, accessibility certification or proof of commercial performance. Record post-launch measurement ownership/review timing when agreed; do not start analytics, monitoring or new data collection without scope. Revisit the brief when observed behavior changes a material assumption rather than treating launch as confirmation of the strategy.
